# HD5 — Primary Evidence Ledger

## Evidence rule

HD5 uses primary empirical studies wherever possible. Formal/philosophical distinctions such
as `content != referent`, `reference != truth` and theory-plural semantics are retained as
conceptual firewalls rather than misrepresented as single-experiment results. Empirical
sources test whether proposed collapses survive modality, development, lesion, comparative
species and AI pressure.

## S01 — Infant statistical segmentation

Saffran, J. R., Aslin, R. N. & Newport, E. L. (1996). **Statistical learning by
8-month-old infants.** Science 274, 1926–1928. PMID `8943209`; DOI
`10.1126/science.274.5294.1926`.

Use: 8-month-old infants segmented continuous artificial speech using statistical relations
after brief exposure. Supports statistical learning as one form-discovery mechanism, not
meaning/reference acquisition.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8943209/

## S02 — Statistical learning extends beyond language

Saffran, J. R., Johnson, E. K., Aslin, R. N. & Newport, E. L. (1999). **Statistical
learning of tone sequences by human infants and adults.** Cognition 70, 27–52. PMID
`10193055`; DOI `10.1016/S0010-0277(98)00075-4`.

Use: comparable sequence-statistics learning with nonlinguistic tone material; rejects
`StatisticalLearning = LanguageSpecificFaculty`.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10193055/

## S03 — Statistical cues have limits

Johnson, E. K. & Tyler, M. D. (2010). **Testing the limits of statistical learning for word
segmentation.** PMID `20136930`.

Use: infants segmented uniform-length artificial words but failed on varying-length words
despite equivalent transitional-probability boundary cues. Supports task-structure-dependent
cue sufficiency.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20136930/

## S04 — Statistical learning in natural language material

Pelucchi, B., Hay, J. F. & Saffran, J. R. (2009). **Statistical learning in a natural
language by 8-month-old infants.** Child Development 80, 674–685. PMID `19489896`; DOI
`10.1111/j.1467-8624.2009.01290.x`.

Use: transitional-probability sensitivity extends to fluent unfamiliar infant-directed
Italian speech under the study design.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19489896/

## S05 — Infant joint reference constrains label mapping

Baldwin, D. A. (1991). **Infants' contribution to the achievement of joint reference.**
Child Development 62, 875–890. PMID `1756664`.

Use: infants do not simply map an heard label to whichever object currently captures their
own focus; social referential evidence matters.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1756664/

## S06 — Infants consult speaker cues for reference

Baldwin, D. A. (1993). **Infants' ability to consult the speaker for clues to word
reference.** Journal of Child Language 20, 395–418. PMID `8376476`; DOI
`10.1017/S0305000900008345`.

Use: speaker focus constrains novel word reference in infant learning; supports
`WordLearning != TemporalCoOccurrenceOnly`.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8376476/

## S07 — Social gating and artificial-language learning, 2026

2026 Nature Communications study **Adult-to-infant unidirectional neural coupling mediates
selective social learning in infants from the United Kingdom and Singapore**.

Use: adult-speaker→infant neural coupling was modulated by eye contact and predicted
selective artificial-language learning. Supports interaction between social attention and
statistical/linguistic learning rather than a false either/or.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-75831-x

## S08 — Homesign emerges without conventional language input

Goldin-Meadow, S. & Mylander, C. (1983). **Gestural communication in deaf children:
noneffect of parental input on language development.** Science 221, 372–374. PMID
`6867713`; DOI `10.1126/science.6867713`.

Use: deaf children without accessible conventional spoken/sign input generated gesture
systems with structural features resembling early language.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6867713/

## S09 — Homesign structure across cultures

Goldin-Meadow, S. & Mylander, C. (1998). **Spontaneous sign systems created by deaf
children in two cultures.** Nature 391, 279–281.

Use: language-like homesign structure recurs across culturally different environments;
strong pressure against deriving all such structure directly from parental gesture input.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/34646

## S10 — Abstract grammatical structure in adult homesign

Coppola, M. & Newport, E. L. (2005). **Grammatical Subjects in home sign: Abstract
linguistic structure in adult primary gesture systems without linguistic input.** PNAS 102,
19249–19253. PMID `16357199`; DOI `10.1073/pnas.0509306102`.

Use: adults sustaining homesign systems show abstract structural regularities; supports
structural generativity without established community language input while not equating
homesign to a full natural language.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16357199/

## S11 — Nicaraguan Sign Language cohort emergence

Senghas, A., Kita, S. & Özyürek, A. (2004). **Children creating core properties of
language: evidence from an emerging sign language in Nicaragua.** Science 305, 1779–1782.
PMID `15375269`; DOI `10.1126/science.1100199`.

Use: successive cohorts segmented and recombined events in increasingly structured ways,
showing that community transmission/learner reconstruction can transform an emerging
linguistic system.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15375269/

## S12 — Productive combinatorial structure in homesign

Goldin-Meadow, S. & Yang, C. (2017). **Statistical evidence that a child can create a
combinatorial linguistic system without external linguistic input.** Neuroscience &
Biobehavioral Reviews. PMID `28041786`.

Use: formal analysis supports productive/combinatorial structure in a child homesign system,
while not establishing all properties of established community languages.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28041786/

## S13 — Sign-language aphasia demonstrates modality independence

Hickok, G., Bellugi, U. & Klima, E. S. (1996). **The neurobiology of sign language and its
implications for the neural basis of language.** Nature 381, 699–702.

Use: lesion evidence in 23 sign-language users supports left-hemisphere language dominance
despite visuo-spatial modality; rejects `Language = Speech/Audition`.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/381699a0

## S14 — Sensory deprivation and sign-language experience dissociate

Cardin, V. et al. (2013). **Dissociating cognitive and sensory neural plasticity in human
superior temporal cortex.** Nature Communications 4, 1473.

Use: sign-language experience and auditory deprivation produced distinguishable cortical
plasticity patterns; linguistic effects cannot be reduced to deafness/visual input.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms2463

## S15 — Naturalistic sign-language hierarchy, 2026

Zimmermann, M. et al. (2026). **Sign language narrative reveals universal and
modality-specific features of cortical timescale hierarchy.** Nature Communications 17,
7126.

Use: Polish Sign Language narrative comprehension shows word/sentence-level frontotemporal
language structure together with modality-specific visual/spatial contributions and broader
plot-level networks.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-73895-3

## S16 — Shared neural language organization across 45 languages

Malik-Moraleda, S. et al. (2022). **An investigation across 45 languages and 12 language
families reveals a universal language network.** Nature Neuroscience 25, 1014–1019. DOI
`10.1038/s41593-022-01114-5`.

Use: robust common topography/lateralization/integration of the language network across
linguistically diverse languages; shared infrastructure does not imply identical grammar or
semantics.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-022-01114-5

## S17 — Grammar not necessary for all causal/mental-state reasoning

Varley, R. & Siegal, M. (2000). **Evidence for cognition without grammar from causal
reasoning and 'theory of mind' in an agrammatic aphasic patient.** Current Biology 10,
723–726. PMID `10873809`; DOI `10.1016/S0960-9822(00)00538-8`.

Use: causal/mental-state reasoning can survive profound grammatical impairment in the
reported patient; rejects language=all reasoning.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10873809/

## S18 — Severe grammar impairment can spare Theory of Mind

Varley, R., Siegal, M. & Want, S. C. (2001). **Severe impairment in grammar does not
preclude theory of mind.** Neurocase 7, 489–493. PMID `11788740`; DOI
`10.1093/neucas/7.6.489`.

Use: replication-style case evidence for functional dissociation.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11788740/

## S19 — Algebra can survive severe aphasia

Klessinger, N., Szczerbinski, M. & Varley, R. (2007). **Algebra in a man with severe
aphasia.** Neuropsychologia. PMID `17306848`; DOI
`10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2007.01.005`.

Use: reported patient could manipulate structure-dependent algebraic expressions despite
severe language impairment; rejects natural language as necessary substrate for all abstract
symbolic cognition.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17306848/

## S20 — Approximate calculation can survive severe global aphasia

Kim, D. Y. et al. (2009). **Dissociation of exact and approximate calculation in severe
global aphasia.** PMID `19452030`.

Use: preserved approximate calculation/numerical comprehension with impaired exact/verbal
calculation in one severe global aphasia case; supports heterogeneous numerical-language
relations.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19452030/

## S21 — Spatial language can scaffold spatial cognition

Gentner, D., Özyürek, A., Gürcanli, Ö. & Goldin-Meadow, S. (2013). **Spatial language
facilitates spatial cognition: evidence from children who lack language input.** Cognition.
PMID `23542409`; DOI `10.1016/j.cognition.2013.01.003`.

Use: homesigners without conventional spatial language show evidence that relational spatial
language can facilitate selected nonlinguistic spatial representations/tasks. Supports
language influence without identity/necessity.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23542409/

## S22 — Spatial language and memory can dissociate across languages

Munnich, E., Landau, B. & Dosher, B. A. (2001). **Spatial language and spatial
representation: a cross-linguistic comparison.** PMID `11483169`.

Use: English/Japanese/Korean language differences did not map straightforwardly onto
nonlinguistic spatial-memory differences; supports partial independence.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11483169/

## S23 — Language/cognition covariation under other spatial ecologies

Haun, D. B. M. et al. (2011). **Plasticity of human spatial cognition: spatial language and
cognition covary across cultures.** Cognition. PMID `21238953`; DOI
`10.1016/j.cognition.2010.12.009`.

Use: Dutch/Namibian spatial-language/cognition alignment under declared tasks. Together with
S22, motivates `LanguageEffect_D != LanguageEffect_E` rather than a universal Whorfian law.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21238953/

## S24 — Experimental compositional syntax in wild bird calls

Suzuki, T. N., Wheatcroft, D. & Griesser, M. (2016). **Experimental evidence for
compositional syntax in bird calls.** Nature Communications 7, 10986. DOI
`10.1038/ncomms10986`.

Use: Japanese great tits extracted distinct responses from ABC/D units and a compound,
order-sensitive response from ABC–D. Rejects `Compositionality = HumanUniqueByDefinition`
under the study's criterion while not equating the call system with Human language.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms10986

## S25 — Additional combinatorial operation in Japanese tits

Suzuki, T. N. & Matsumoto, Y. K. (2022). **Experimental evidence for core-Merge in the
vocal communication system of a wild passerine.** Nature Communications 13, 5605.

Use: further comparative pressure on claims that all meaningful combination operations are
Human-exclusive; property-specific interpretation remains mandatory.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-33360-3

## S26 — Reference is collaboratively stabilized

Clark, H. H. & Wilkes-Gibbs, D. (1986). **Referring as a collaborative process.**
Cognition 22, 1–39. PMID `3709088`; DOI `10.1016/0010-0277(86)90010-7`.

Use: referring expressions are interactively established/refined rather than simple
context-free word→object lookups.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3709088/

## S27 — Partner-specific conceptual pacts

Brennan, S. E. & Clark, H. H. (1996). **Conceptual pacts and lexical choice in
conversation.** PMID `8921603`.

Use: speakers retain partner-specific conceptualizations in later references and adapt when
partners change; supports local convention/common-ground interaction with lexical choice.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8921603/

## S28 — Online nonlinguistic context shapes syntactic/reference interpretation

Tanenhaus, M. K., Spivey-Knowlton, M. J., Eberhard, K. M. & Sedivy, J. C. (1995).
**Integration of visual and linguistic information in spoken language comprehension.**
Science 268, 1632–1634. PMID `7777863`; DOI `10.1126/science.7777863`.

Use: visual context immediately affects interpretation of temporally ambiguous spoken
instructions; rejects text-only sequential decode accounts.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777863/

## S29 — Semantics/pragmatics developmental dissociation

Papafragou, A. & Musolino, J. (2003). **Scalar implicatures: experiments at the
semantics-pragmatics interface.** Cognition. PMID `12485740`; DOI
`10.1016/S0010-0277(02)00179-8`.

Use: preschool children often accept semantically true but pragmatically underinformative
scalar statements that adults reject; task manipulations alter pragmatic inferences.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12485740/

## S30 — Real-time child semantic/pragmatic distinction

Huang, Y. T. & Snedeker, J. (2009). **Semantic meaning and pragmatic interpretation in
5-year-olds: evidence from real-time spoken language comprehension.** PMID `19899927`.

Use: children rapidly respect semantic constraints while failing adult-like scalar pragmatic
inference under the tested online paradigm.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19899927/

## S31 — Literacy reorganizes visual/language cortical networks

Dehaene, S. et al. (2010). **How learning to read changes the cortical networks for vision
and language.** Science 330, 1359–1364. PMID `21071632`; DOI
`10.1126/science.1194140`.

Use: adult illiterates, adult-acquired readers and childhood readers show literacy-dependent
changes in visual and language-related responses. Supports writing/literacy as an external
symbolic technology that can reshape cognition/brain while remaining distinct from language
itself.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21071632/

## S32 — Longitudinal adult literacy changes visual-system connectivity

Skeide, M. A. et al. (2017). **Learning to read alters cortico-subcortical cross-talk in the
visual system of illiterates.** PMID `28560333`.

Use: six months of adult literacy training produced measurable connectivity changes related
to decoding gains; causal/developmental pressure for external symbolic skill plasticity.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28560333/

## S33 — Text-only LLMs recover some, not all, Human concept structure

2025 Nature Human Behaviour: **Large language models without grounding recover
non-sensorimotor but not sensorimotor features of human concepts.**

Use: comparison across ~4,442 lexical concepts found strongest Human-model alignment on
non-sensorimotor dimensions, weaker sensory and lowest motor alignment; visual learning
improved visual-related dimensions. Supports multidimensional grounding rather than all-or-
nothing claims.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02203-8

## S34 — LLM-derived representations predict and manipulate Human language-network response

Tuckute, G. et al. (2024). **Driving and suppressing the human language network using large
language models.** Nature Human Behaviour.

Use: GPT-based encoding model predicted sentence-level Human language-network response and
selected novel sentences that experimentally drove/suppressed that activity. Supports shared
predictive structure while not proving algorithm/representation/experience identity.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01783-7

## S35 — Language and Theory-of-Mind neural systems are dissociable early in development

Hiersche, K. J., Osher, D. E. & Saygin, Z. M. (2026). **Functional dissociation of
language and theory of mind in the developing superior temporal lobe.** Communications
Biology 9, 558.

Use: language- and ToM-selective patterns/connectivity fingerprints are dissociable in
children as young as the studied 3-year range and in adults, strengthening the nonidentity
of language/social cognition.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-026-10040-2

## S36 — Spoken language has separable meaning/form and recognition/production dimensions

Butler, R. A. et al. (2014/2015). **Neural organization of spoken language revealed by
lesion–symptom mapping.** Nature Communications.

Use: 99 chronic aphasia participants show separable semantic/phonological and
recognition/production dimensions; rejects one language faculty scalar at the neural/
functional level.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms7762

# Evidence boundary

The ledger does **not** establish:

```text
one semantic theory
one syntax formalism
one semantics-pragmatics cut
one universal acquisition mechanism
one universal linguistic feature set
one Human-unique compositional mechanism
one deterministic language→thought law
one definition of AI semantic understanding
one proof that sensorimotor grounding implies experience
```

Its role is to pressure the boundary between generic representation, communication events,
persistent conventions/cultural transmission and the newly admitted generative symbolic-
system layer.
