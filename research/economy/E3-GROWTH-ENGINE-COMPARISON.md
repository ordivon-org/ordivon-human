# E3 — Comparing Personal Wealth Growth Engines

## 1. Question

> At each stage of personal wealth formation, which mechanism can change the trajectory by the largest realistic amount relative to time, life-quality cost, reversibility, and failure risk?

E3 compares mechanisms from first principles and uses public averages only to calibrate scale.

It does not infer individual causality from population data.

## 2. The competing engines

A person's controllable wealth can expand through:

1. higher captured labour or business income;
2. a larger quality-preserving surplus;
3. ownership of products, systems, or enterprises;
4. return on accumulated capital;
5. external transfers;
6. leverage that multiplies one of the above.

These mechanisms are not interchangeable.

## 3. Capital return is proportional to the existing base

For an owned capital base `K` and net return rate `r`:

```text
annual capital gain or income ≈ K × r
```

Therefore the capital required for investment return to equal a recurring annual income increase `ΔY` is:

```text
K* = ΔY / r
```

This is an identity, not a return forecast.

### Illustrative equivalence

| Recurring annual income increase | Capital required at 5% | at 8% | at 10% |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 12,000 yuan | 240,000 | 150,000 | 120,000 |
| 24,000 yuan | 480,000 | 300,000 | 240,000 |
| 60,000 yuan | 1,200,000 | 750,000 | 600,000 |

A recurring 1,000-yuan monthly increase has the same one-year magnitude as an 8% return on 150,000 yuan of capital.

The table does not show that the income increase is easy, permanent, or costless. It shows why investment return has limited absolute power when the capital base is small.

## 4. Public-average calibration

China's official 2025 household release reports:

- per-capita disposable income: 43,377 yuan;
- wages and salaries: 24,555 yuan, or 56.6% of disposable income;
- net business income: 7,252 yuan, or 16.7%;
- property income: 3,490 yuan, or 8.0%;
- per-capita consumption expenditure: 29,476 yuan.

These averages do not describe one typical person and do not prove a strategy.

They establish two useful scale constraints:

1. labour and business channels are much larger than property income in the current average income structure;
2. an annual 12,000-yuan income change is economically large relative to the public per-capita baseline, while the same amount requires a six-figure capital base at ordinary illustrative return rates.

The average income–consumption difference of 13,901 yuan is not treated as a household saving estimate. It is used only as a rough annual-flow magnitude.

## 5. Recurring surplus versus return optimization

With initial capital `K0`, annual surplus `S`, annual net return `r`, and `n` periods, a simplified end wealth is:

```text
W_n
=
K0(1+r)^n
+
S × ((1+r)^n − 1) / r
```

This model assumes constant annual surplus and return, ignores taxes and volatility, and is used only to compare mechanism scale.

### Ten-year illustration

Assume:

- initial capital: 10,000 yuan;
- annual surplus: 12,000 yuan;
- ten years;
- end-of-year contributions.

| Scenario | End wealth |
|---|---:|
| 5% net return | 167,224 yuan |
| 8% net return | 195,428 yuan |
| 10% net return | 217,187 yuan |
| 8% return and 24,000 annual surplus | 369,267 yuan |

Under these assumptions:

- raising return from 8% to 10% adds about 21,759 yuan;
- raising annual surplus from 12,000 to 24,000 adds about 173,839 yuan.

This does not mean higher surplus is always preferable. Increasing surplus by harming health, learning, or life quality may destroy the mechanism that produced it.

It means that at a low capital base, recurring flow changes can dominate moderate return optimization by an order of magnitude.

## 6. Engine A — Cost reduction

### Mechanism

```text
lower consumption or maintenance
→ larger retained surplus
```

### Strength

- immediate;
- relatively controllable;
- no market permission required.

### Hard limit

Cost reduction is bounded by existing expenditure and the life-quality floor.

Even perfect elimination of low-value spending cannot grow beyond the amount being spent. It does not directly increase value creation, capture, or scalability.

### Correct role

- remove low-value leakage;
- avoid harmful fixed commitments;
- fund capability and ownership formation.

### Failure mode

Treating deprivation as productivity and cutting health, tools, education, relationships, or recovery that sustain future value creation.

## 7. Engine B — Earning and capture growth

### Mechanism

```text
more valuable capability
+ better market access
+ stronger proof and bargaining
→ larger recurring captured value
```

### Strength

A recurring flow improvement repeats every period and can finance all later engines.

### Limits

- requires time and uncertainty;
- may demand relocation, learning, or market change;
- can increase stress and work dependence;
- salary growth may be captured by lifestyle expansion.

### Stage judgment

This is usually the dominant internally generated engine when:

- capital is small;
- labour income is the main current resource source;
- capability can still move to higher-value markets;
- a plausible income change exceeds likely capital return in absolute terms.

## 8. Engine C — Productive ownership

### Mechanism

```text
surplus or creative work
→ product, code, system, business, distribution, or equity
→ future residual claims
```

### Strength

Ownership can break the proportional relation between current hours and future income.

### Limits

- uncertain demand;
- high failure rate before useful scale;
- maintenance and concentration;
- legal, platform, and governance dependence.

### Stage judgment

Ownership is the main transition mechanism between labour-dominant income and capital- or system-dominant wealth.

It can matter before large financial capital exists because code, media, products, and reputation may be created partly through labour. But the path is less predictable than ordinary employment.

## 9. Engine D — Capital compounding

### Mechanism

```text
owned productive claims
→ return
→ additional owned claims
```

### Strength

- low marginal personal labour when diversified;
- automatic reinvestment can compound for long periods;
- useful for converting temporary surplus into durable claims.

### Limits

- absolute effect is proportional to capital;
- return is uncertain;
- concentration or leverage can create permanent loss;
- optimizing small return differences can consume attention disproportionate to economic value.

### Stage judgment

Capital compounding is always useful when reasonably priced and diversified productive claims are available, but it becomes a dominant engine only after the base is large relative to new labour surplus and consumption needs.

## 10. Engine E — Productive leverage

### Mechanism

```text
one unit of effort
→ tools, replication, distribution, organization, or capital
→ many units of output
```

### Strength

Leverage can change the order of magnitude rather than merely improve a percentage.

### Limits

- the lever may amplify bad product selection or weak demand;
- organizational leverage creates coordination cost;
- platform leverage creates dependency;
- financial leverage creates ruin risk.

### Stage judgment

The highest-upside paths generally contain leverage, but leverage should be evaluated by whether it creates a durable productive base, not by gross scale alone.

## 11. Engine F — External transfers

Inheritance, family support, grants, and windfalls can move a person between stages immediately.

They must be represented because they change the starting state. They are not an internally reproducible wealth-formation strategy.

Control conditions matter: a nominal resource controlled by another person may not expand autonomy.

## 12. Mechanism ranking by stage

| Stage | Usually highest-leverage question | Secondary engine | Main danger |
|---|---|---|---|
| dependence | can capability become independently exchangeable? | low-cost experimentation | speculation replacing capability |
| labour-dominant income | can recurring captured value rise materially? | cost efficiency and proof | optimizing tiny capital returns |
| stable surplus | can surplus buy capability and ownership? | diversified capital accumulation | lifestyle absorption or one large bet |
| scalable ownership | can output grow without proportional owner time? | organization and capital | founder overload and concentration |
| capital-dominant | can productive claims be preserved and redeployed? | selective creation | permanent loss and complexity |
| high autonomy | does more accumulation expand valued options? | stewardship | wealth maintenance becoming the new compulsion |

This table is a default hypothesis, not a personal prescription.

## 13. Decision test for a proposed wealth action

For any action, ask:

1. Which engine does it improve?
2. What is the plausible annual or lifetime magnitude?
3. Is the effect recurring, one-time, or merely a valuation change?
4. What existing base does it multiply?
5. Does it create ownership or only more labour?
6. What life-quality and attention costs does it impose?
7. What ordinary failure mode ends the path?
8. Can the action be reversed before ruin?
9. Which higher-value engine does it displace?

A strategy should be rejected when its expected effect is small relative to the opportunity cost of attention, even if the percentage return looks impressive.

## 14. E3 result

The main first-principles conclusion is stage-dependent:

```text
small capital
→ capability, market access, captured income, and ownership formation dominate

stable surplus
→ conversion into scalable ownership and diversified productive claims dominates

large productive base
→ capital allocation, preservation, and compounding become dominant
```

The crucial transition is not from low return to high return.

It is from:

```text
finite labour
→ recurring surplus
→ productive ownership
→ leveraged and compounding claims
```

The next admitted research should examine how present expenditure can either support this transition or consume it: the life-quality and capability-investment frontier.
