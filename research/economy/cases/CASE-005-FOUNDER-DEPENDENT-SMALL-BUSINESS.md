# CASE-005 — Founder-Dependent Small Business

> **Evidence status:** closed composite structural pressure test.
>
> **Primary modules:** E0 and E5–E9. Other E sections test guardrails and false recommendations; they are not equally diagnostic.

> Return to the [case index](README.md) or apply the framework through the [practice guide](../PRACTICE-GUIDE.md).

## 0. Case boundary

This synthetic case contains no identifying business type, location, dates, founder age, revenue, staff count, customer, platform, or asset amount.

It tests whether E0–E9 can distinguish business growth from owner-independent productive leverage.

## 1. Generalized starting state

| Dimension | Structural condition |
|---|---|
| demand | repeated and commercially meaningful |
| revenue | sufficient to support the owner and a small operating system |
| team | several people or contractors participate |
| owner role | owner controls sales, key relationships, quality, exceptions, and urgent decisions |
| process | normal work is partly documented, but tacit knowledge dominates difficult cases |
| concentration | income, business equity, identity, and professional network share the same failure |
| capital | much of accumulated value remains inside the business |
| obligations | staff, suppliers, customers, and fixed costs reduce reversibility |
| autonomy | nominal control is high; actual ability to leave or refuse is low |
| risk | growth can increase revenue while increasing owner burden and correlated failure |

## 2. E0 diagnosis

The first unsupported transition is:

```text
valuable founder-led business
→ repeatable governed production
that survives bounded owner absence
```

The binding constraint is founder dependence, not demand generation alone.

The model should rank reliability and separation above faster expansion until the normal system is visible.

## 3. Action ranking

1. preserve service quality, staff obligations, and continuity;
2. identify the most expensive recurring founder dependency;
3. define the normal case and escalation boundary;
4. transfer one responsibility through supervised evidence;
5. test bounded owner absence;
6. separate personal continuity capital from operating capital;
7. diversify only as control and marginal internal opportunity decline;
8. resume growth when support burden and failure risk are understood.

## 4. E1 — preserve the founder’s judgment without centralizing everything

Founder capability often contains:

- customer trust;
- product judgment;
- exception handling;
- technical or craft knowledge;
- hiring and coordination;
- capital allocation;
- tacit history.

The goal is not to delete founder judgment. It is to identify which decisions genuinely benefit from it and which remain centralized only because they were never specified.

### Judgment map

For each recurring founder intervention, classify:

- routine decision with clear inputs;
- judgment that can be reviewed;
- exceptional decision requiring founder context;
- authority retained for control rather than value;
- problem caused by poor process or incentives.

## 5. E2 — choose the desired business relation

### Path A — intentionally owner-operated firm

Valid when the owner enjoys direct operation, scale remains bounded, and the business supports the desired life.

### Path B — managed operating business

Requires documented normal work, accountable operators, information access, and governance.

### Path C — transferable enterprise

Requires owner-independent demand, process, records, and rights that another party can evaluate and control.

### Path D — harvest and simplify

Valid when marginal growth creates little autonomy and the owner prefers lower complexity or partial extraction.

The model should not treat maximum enterprise size as the universal objective.

## 6. E3 — demand proof must be separated from founder proof

Current customers may trust the founder personally rather than the organization.

Test whether the business—not only the founder—can produce trust through:

- a consistent offer;
- visible quality standards;
- team-delivered normal cases;
- documented customer communication;
- independent references;
- recurring contracts or repeat use not requiring founder intervention.

### Failure signal

If customers return only when the founder personally sells, delivers, and resolves issues, demand is not yet organizationally owned.

## 7. E4 — measure owner-adjusted economics

Accounting profit can overstate productive ownership when the founder provides unpaid management, sales, emergency work, and reputation.

A more useful calculation is:

```text
business surplus
− market cost of founder labour
− recovery and emergency burden
− deferred maintenance
− concentration cost
```

The purpose is not precise valuation. It is to determine whether the business produces residual value beyond a demanding owner job.

## 8. E5 — protect life and prevent commitment escalation

Growth can create irreversible obligations before repeatability is proven:

- long leases;
- permanent staffing;
- debt or guarantees;
- inventory or infrastructure;
- service promises;
- owner availability expectations.

### Commitment gate

Before adding fixed capacity, require:

- repeated demand;
- stable unit economics;
- a defined normal process;
- a responsible operator;
- survivable downside;
- an exit or reduction path.

Do not use permanent commitments to discover whether the business model works.

## 9. E6 — ownership quality

The founder may legally own the business while lacking usable control because information and operation are trapped in personal attention.

Productive ownership requires:

- residual income beyond owner labour;
- reliable information;
- rights over customer, brand, process, and assets;
- transferable records;
- controlled liabilities;
- the ability to appoint, replace, or review operators;
- bounded owner absence.

### Platform and customer concentration

One customer, platform, supplier, or channel can make business ownership revocable in practice. The goal is not perfect independence, but no single unpriced dependency should silently determine survival.

## 10. E7 — remove one founder bottleneck

### Transfer sequence

```text
observe founder intervention
→ define expected result
→ document normal path
→ state exception boundary
→ train one operator
→ supervise several cycles
→ test without intervention
→ review failure
```

Choose only one bottleneck for the first cycle.

Good candidates are frequent, expensive, observable, and not the source of the founder’s unique advantage.

### Owner-absence test

Run a bounded absence from one normal operating cycle with:

- explicit escalation rules;
- access to required information;
- no hidden real-time rescue;
- a post-test record of failures.

The objective is not a vacation performance. It is evidence about where the system still depends on the founder.

### Scaling rule

Resume material expansion only when:

- the normal case works repeatedly;
- support and error rates remain acceptable;
- owner time per unit is falling;
- staff authority and incentives are clear;
- one failure does not endanger the entire business.

## 11. E8 — capital by function

Separate at least conceptually:

1. personal continuity capital;
2. operating liquidity;
3. growth capital tied to a named proven mechanism;
4. protection against liabilities and concentration;
5. independent diversified claims outside the business.

### Reinvestment test

Continue concentrated reinvestment only when the next unit has a clear controlled productive use and the owner’s whole economic state can survive failure.

### Diversification trigger

Increase independent capital when:

- marginal internal opportunities weaken;
- owner dependence remains high;
- personal life and business share the same failure;
- control can be retained with less economic exposure;
- liquidity and refusal power are insufficient.

No fixed extraction percentage follows.

## 12. E9 — bounded validation cycle

### Phase A — dependency map

- list recurring founder interventions;
- identify customer, platform, supplier, and operator concentration;
- estimate hidden founder labour;
- separate operating and personal continuity needs.

### Phase B — one transfer

- select one normal responsibility;
- define result, process, check, and escalation;
- supervise repeated delivery;
- record failures without expanding scope.

### Phase C — owner-absence test

- remove the founder from one bounded cycle;
- allow only declared escalation;
- measure quality, delay, customer response, and hidden intervention.

### Phase D — capital and growth decision

- scale when repeatability and owner leverage improve;
- redesign when the operator lacks information or authority;
- retain owner operation intentionally when transfer cost exceeds value;
- simplify or extract capital when growth reduces autonomy.

## 13. Evidence threshold

The case progresses when most of the following appear:

- one responsibility operates without continuous founder rescue;
- normal process and exceptions are explicit;
- owner time per unit falls;
- customer trust survives team delivery;
- operating and personal continuity are separated;
- one concentration risk is reduced or explicitly priced;
- life quality improves or remains stable.

## 14. Competing actions rejected

### Add more customers immediately

Rejected when delivery and customer trust remain founder-bound.

### Hire a senior operator to solve everything

Rejected when authority, information, economics, and expected results are undefined.

### Automate every process

Rejected because unstable processes and exception-heavy work should not be automated blindly.

### Keep all wealth inside the business

Rejected when the owner’s life, income, identity, and capital already fail together and marginal internal opportunities are weak.

### Sell the business immediately

Rejected when transferability is unproven and the owner may destroy value by treating exhaustion as a valuation signal.

## 15. Model pressure points

### Ownership can increase compulsion

A founder may have more nominal control than an employee while possessing less usable time and refusal power.

### Delegation can create negative leverage

More people can increase communication, supervision, and error faster than output.

### Concentration may remain rational

The model must preserve concentrated creation where the founder still has strong control and high marginal opportunities.

## 16. Initial verdict

```text
one proven owner-independent operating unit
>
faster growth, broad delegation, or automatic diversification
```

The case supports the E-series only if it can distinguish intentional owner operation from failed systemization and can recommend concentration or extraction conditionally rather than ideologically.
