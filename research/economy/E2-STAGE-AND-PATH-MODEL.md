# E2 — Stage and Path Model of Personal Wealth Formation

## 1. Purpose

E2 identifies which wealth mechanism is likely to dominate at different starting states.

The model is organized by binding constraint, not age, occupation, or one wealth threshold.

A person's stage changes when the mechanism limiting further economic power changes.

## 2. Stage 0 — Economic dependence

### State

- little or no independently controlled income;
- limited capital;
- economic choices depend on family, institution, or another controller;
- time may be available, but market proof and access are weak.

### Binding constraints

- authority;
- basic capability;
- evidence of reliability;
- access to paying demand.

### Dominant objective

```text
create the first independently exchangeable capability
```

### High-value moves

- acquire a capability with real demand;
- produce visible proof through work, projects, or credentials where they matter;
- enter environments with stronger learning and market access;
- preserve low-cost experimentation;
- use tools and AI to increase practice and output.

### Common error

Treating small speculative capital as the primary path to independence before reliable value creation exists.

## 3. Stage 1 — Labour-dominant independent income

### State

- independent income exists;
- most income depends on current labour;
- surplus is small or inconsistent;
- losing one employer or client materially changes the trajectory.

### Binding constraints

- unit value of time;
- market access;
- bargaining power;
- income reliability;
- cost of maintaining life and capability.

### Dominant objective

```text
increase durable captured value per unit of time
```

### High-value moves

- move toward higher-value problems and markets;
- improve proof, distribution, and negotiation;
- build transferable rather than employer-specific capability;
- reduce low-value work through tools and automation;
- avoid commitments that force acceptance of any available income.

### Common error

Optimizing a portfolio return on a capital base too small to rival plausible income growth.

## 4. Stage 2 — Stable surplus and conversion

### State

- income reliably exceeds the cost of a quality-preserving life;
- a repeatable surplus exists;
- some failure can be absorbed;
- the person can allocate resources beyond immediate survival.

### Binding constraints

- converting surplus into productive capability and ownership;
- choosing between safety, exploration, and long-horizon claims;
- preventing lifestyle growth from absorbing all gains.

### Dominant objective

```text
turn recurring surplus into a larger future production base
```

### High-value moves

- reinvest in capability with visible market conversion;
- build reusable code, products, media, systems, or distribution;
- acquire diversified productive assets;
- fund reversible experiments;
- maintain enough resilience that failure does not terminate the path.

### Common error

Accumulating surplus without creating any ownership, or concentrating all surplus in one fragile bet.

## 5. Stage 3 — Scalable ownership

### State

- part of income comes from owned products, systems, business equity, intellectual property, distribution, or capital;
- output is no longer strictly proportional to current hours;
- growth depends on demand, replication, organization, and maintenance.

### Binding constraints

- product–market fit;
- distribution;
- organizational quality;
- capital allocation;
- dependence on one product, platform, or market.

### Dominant objective

```text
increase the durable value produced per unit of owner attention
```

### High-value moves

- strengthen product quality and recurring demand;
- reduce marginal delivery cost;
- create systems that survive the founder's temporary absence;
- diversify customer, platform, and key-person dependencies;
- convert volatile business gains into broader durable claims.

### Common error

Calling self-employment scalable ownership when income still stops immediately with the owner's labour.

## 6. Stage 4 — Capital- and system-dominant wealth

### State

- owned productive claims contribute materially to total resource growth;
- labour income is no longer the sole support of life;
- capital preservation, allocation, governance, and concentration become first-order concerns.

### Binding constraints

- avoiding large permanent loss;
- concentration and correlated failure;
- governance and control;
- tax, transaction, and maintenance drag;
- deciding what wealth is for.

### Dominant objective

```text
preserve and redeploy productive claims while converting wealth into time and choice
```

### Common error

Continuing to optimize accumulation after additional wealth no longer meaningfully expands valued options, while maintenance and complexity increase.

## 7. Stage 5 — High economic autonomy

### State

- essential life does not require accepting ordinary market offers;
- the person can choose work for meaning, influence, curiosity, or further creation;
- financial survival is not the dominant allocator of time.

### Binding constraints

- purpose;
- health and finite life;
- stewardship and responsibility;
- maintaining real capability rather than passive dependence on wealth managers or institutions.

This is not a mandatory retirement state. Continued work may be freely chosen.

## 8. Stage transitions

### T0 → T1: capability becomes exchangeable

Required change:

```text
private learning
→ credible result for a paying market
```

### T1 → T2: income becomes reliable surplus

Required change:

```text
captured value growth
>
quality-preserving cost growth
```

### T2 → T3: surplus becomes scalable ownership

Required change:

```text
repeated saving or labour
→ claims that continue producing beyond the original effort
```

### T3 → T4: owned systems become a major growth engine

Required change:

```text
return from owned productive claims
becomes material relative to labour income and consumption needs
```

### T4 → T5: wealth becomes usable freedom

Required change:

```text
resources and control
→ durable ability to choose rather than merely continue accumulating
```

## 9. Main path families

### P1 — Professional capability compounding

```text
skill
→ stronger proof
→ higher-value work
→ bargaining power
→ surplus
→ diversified ownership
```

Strengths:

- low initial capital;
- relatively reversible;
- compatible with gradual learning.

Limits:

- labour remains central for a long period;
- market and employer structure may cap capture.

### P2 — Tool-leveraged independent production

```text
capability
→ tools, code, and AI
→ more output per person
→ service or product ownership
→ replication
```

Strengths:

- low organizational overhead;
- increasing feasibility in software and digital work.

Limits:

- distribution and demand remain hard;
- tool advantage may diffuse quickly.

### P3 — Entrepreneurship and organization

```text
problem selection
→ product
→ customers
→ organization
→ residual ownership
```

Strengths:

- high possible value creation and capture;
- strong organizational and capital leverage.

Limits:

- high variance;
- coordination, financing, and market risk;
- may consume life quality and autonomy before succeeding.

### P4 — Income-led capital accumulation

```text
labour or business surplus
→ diversified productive assets
→ long-horizon compounding
```

Strengths:

- simple;
- low maintenance when diversified;
- does not require building an organization.

Limits:

- slow when initial surplus is small;
- cannot substitute for weak value creation in early stages.

### P5 — External transfer

```text
inheritance, family transfer, grant, or windfall
→ immediate resource base
```

This can move a person between stages, but it is not an internally generated mechanism and may carry control conditions.

### P6 — Speculation and concentrated bets

```text
small capital
→ high variance exposure
→ possible rapid gain or loss
```

This can change state quickly, but it does not reliably create productive capability or ownership. It is treated as a contingent risk-taking path, not the default wealth engine.

## 10. Life-quality interaction

Each stage has a different quality-of-life risk:

| Stage | Main risk to life quality |
|---|---|
| dependence | low authority and constrained choice |
| labour-dominant | time exhaustion and employer dependence |
| surplus conversion | over-saving or endless preparation |
| scalable ownership | founder overload and uncertain demand |
| capital-dominant | complexity, vigilance, and purpose loss |
| high autonomy | capability decay or passive detachment |

A path fails the program even when wealth rises if it permanently destroys the present-life floor or narrows the person's future action set.

## 11. Risk and liquidity are support constraints

The prior E2 made household liquidity the central research object. The revised model places it correctly:

```text
wealth-formation path
subject to
survival, liquidity, commitment, control, and recovery constraints
```

A thin support rule is retained:

> Do not adopt a growth path whose ordinary failure mode prevents continuation, recovery, or the preservation of essential life.

No CHFS/CFPS microdata program is required to retain this rule.

## 12. E2 result

The same action changes sign across stages.

- Additional learning may dominate at Stage 0 but become avoidance at Stage 2.
- Income growth may dominate investment return at Stage 1.
- Ownership formation may dominate salary optimization at Stage 2 or 3.
- Diversification and preservation may dominate aggressive creation at Stage 4.
- Time and purpose may dominate further accumulation at Stage 5.

Therefore the next research question is not "which strategy is best?"

It is:

> At each stage, which mechanism can change the person's trajectory by the largest realistic amount relative to its time, life-quality cost, reversibility, and failure risk?
