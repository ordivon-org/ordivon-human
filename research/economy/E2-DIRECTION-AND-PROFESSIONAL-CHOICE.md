# E2 — Choose a Direction by Testing Reality

> **Framework role:** work-and-exchange direction test.
>
> **Use this module when:** a major education or professional commitment has not yet earned evidence from real work. This is a selectable decision module, not a compulsory life stage. Start with the [practice guide](PRACTICE-GUIDE.md).

## What this chapter is for

A professional direction should not be selected only from interest, salary rankings, prestige, or fear of missing out. It should be selected through bounded contact with the work, the market, and the cost of entry.

The goal is not to identify one permanent identity. It is to choose the next direction in which deeper investment is justified.

## The six tests for a direction

For every candidate path, answer:

1. **Problem** — what useful result does this field produce?
2. **Demand** — who needs that result and can allocate resources to it?
3. **Proof** — can a beginner produce inspectable evidence before full entry?
4. **Access** — what credentials, geography, language, network, or capital are required?
5. **Transferability** — what remains valuable if the employer, tool, platform, or field changes?
6. **Cost and reversibility** — how much time, debt, and lost opportunity are required before the path can be tested?

A path with high theoretical income but no realistic access may be weaker than a lower-status path with direct proof, strong demand, and room to improve.

## Use three layers, not one irreversible choice

### Layer 1 — broad sampling

Sample several domains cheaply through introductory work, conversations, and small outputs.

### Layer 2 — primary capability track

Choose one capability that can become deep enough to exchange. This receives most professional learning time.

### Layer 3 — complementary leverage

Add one or two capabilities that expand the primary track, such as:

- language;
- writing and explanation;
- data and computation;
- AI-assisted production and verification;
- sales and distribution;
- project coordination.

Do not maintain five “primary” tracks. That usually produces shallow familiarity and no market proof.

## A six-week direction test

Before a major commitment, run a small test:

### Week 1 — observe the work

- inspect real job descriptions, project briefs, or client requests;
- speak with at least two people doing the work;
- identify routine tasks, difficult tasks, and entry barriers.

### Weeks 2–4 — produce a small result

Complete one task that resembles the field’s real output. Avoid a tutorial whose answer is already supplied.

### Week 5 — expose it

Ask a practitioner, user, teacher, or potential buyer to inspect the result.

### Week 6 — decide

Record:

- what was difficult;
- whether the difficulty became more interesting or only more aversive;
- what skill gap appeared;
- whether access is realistic;
- what the next six months would cost;
- what useful capability remains if the path is abandoned.

The test does not prove a lifetime fit. It determines whether deeper investment has earned another stage.

## Case 1 — Choosing computer science because it appears powerful

**Situation.** Mei is 18 and considering computer science because AI and software appear to offer high upside. She has used applications extensively but has never written a program or solved a technical problem independently.

**Tempting move.** Commit immediately based on industry narratives, or reject the field after one difficult introductory lesson.

**Practical move.** Run a six-week test: learn basic programming, build a small tool for a real personal or school task, debug it without copying a complete solution, and ask an experienced student to review the result. At the same time, inspect actual entry roles rather than only famous companies.

**Why this is realistic.** The test is cheap and produces transferable logic and digital skills even if computer science is not selected.

**Failure signal.** Finishing copied tutorials without being able to modify or explain them is not evidence of fit.

## Case 2 — Vocational capability versus a generic degree

**Situation.** Jun is 17, performs moderately in academic subjects, enjoys repairing equipment, and has access to a credible technical program connected to local industrial employers. The family prefers any university degree because it appears safer and more prestigious.

**Tempting move.** Choose solely by social status, or assume hands-on interest automatically guarantees a good technical career.

**Practical move.** Compare complete paths. Visit the training environment, inspect equipment and curriculum, speak with recent graduates and employers, and test one practical module. Compare this with the specific university program’s teaching quality, completion cost, internship access, and graduate outcomes.

**Why this is realistic.** The decision is not “degree versus no degree” in the abstract. It compares two actual access systems, both with possible strengths and failures.

**Failure signal.** A program that advertises employment but cannot show competent instruction, real equipment, or credible employer links should not receive trust merely because the occupation is useful.

## Case 3 — Expensive postgraduate study as an escape from uncertainty

**Situation.** Rui is finishing university and is unsure about employment. A postgraduate program abroad offers language, migration, network, and credential opportunities, but requires substantial family resources or debt. Rui has little direct experience in the target profession.

**Tempting move.** Treat the degree as automatically valuable because the destination and institution are prestigious, or reject it because education has no guaranteed return.

**Practical move.** Separate the value components: qualification, market access, language, location, network, and time. Verify which target roles require the degree, what proof can be built before enrollment, whether the family can absorb a weak outcome, and what lower-cost route could test the same profession first.

**Why this is realistic.** Education can be a high-value access investment, but only when the access mechanism and downside are explicit. The test does not demand certainty; it prevents an expensive commitment from substituting for direction discovery.

**Failure signal.** If the decision remains justified only by a broad statement such as “better opportunities,” the mechanism is still too vague.

## The direction evidence sheet

For each serious candidate, keep one page:

| Item | Evidence required |
|---|---|
| real output | one example of what competent work produces |
| beginner test | one result personally completed |
| demand | actual roles, clients, or institutional needs |
| access | required credentials, language, location, and network |
| transferable base | capabilities that survive a path change |
| full cost | tuition, time, debt, relocation, and lost alternatives |
| next checkpoint | evidence needed before deeper commitment |
| exit value | what remains if the path is stopped |

Do not score twenty dimensions. The sheet exists to expose missing mechanisms.

## When to specialize further

Increase commitment when:

- the work has been sampled directly;
- the learner can tolerate and improve at its difficult parts;
- real demand is visible;
- proof can be built;
- access costs are acceptable;
- the path preserves useful capabilities even under failure.

Reduce or stop commitment when:

- progress depends on a credential with no verified access value;
- the actual work is persistently unlike the imagined work;
- entry requires an unrecoverable financial commitment;
- the field offers no realistic proof path;
- another path produces stronger evidence at lower cost.

## E2 result

A good direction is not the one with the most attractive description. It is the one that survives direct contact with real work, real demand, real access constraints, and the person’s actual ability to improve.
