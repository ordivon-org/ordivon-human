# H0-F — Capability

## Definition and boundary

Capability denotes a person's reliable ability to achieve a class of outcomes under declared conditions, including the ability to adapt when the task, environment, or available means change.

It is not identical to:

- biological or cognitive potential;
- one observed performance;
- formal qualification;
- knowledge without execution;
- effort or motivation;
- access to an opportunity;
- output produced mainly by another person or machine;
- moral worth.

Capability is relational. What a person can do depends partly on body, cognition, emotion, knowledge, practice, tools, relationships, institutions, and task conditions.

## Core components

| Component | Working scope |
|---|---|
| foundational functioning | bodily, sensory, cognitive, emotional, and social prerequisites |
| knowledge and representation | facts, concepts, procedures, domain models, and language |
| skill and automatization | learned performance that becomes reliable and efficient |
| execution | turning intention and plan into completed action |
| judgment and verification | selecting methods, detecting error, calibrating confidence, checking consequences |
| transfer | applying learning to materially different tasks or contexts |
| adaptation | revising strategy when conditions, tools, or constraints change |
| coordination | combining capability across people, organizations, and agents |
| tool integration | using external representations, machines, software, and AI effectively |
| resilience and recovery | continuing or rebuilding after interruption, failure, resource loss, or change |

P0 does not assume these components form one latent capability score.

## Capability layers

The same person may have different values at several layers:

```text
underlying capacity
available capacity in the current bodily and emotional state
independent skill without external assistance
situated capability with ordinary tools and resources
joint-system capability with people, organizations, or AI
observed performance on one task
long-term transfer and recovery capability
```

These layers prevent two common errors:

1. mistaking low performance under poor conditions for absent ability;
2. mistaking a strong supported result for fully internalized human skill.

## Functions

Capability allows a person to:

- satisfy needs and maintain functioning;
- learn and solve problems;
- create, communicate, and coordinate;
- work within and alter environments;
- convert resources into valued outcomes;
- use tools and other people without losing authorship where authorship matters;
- respond to novelty, uncertainty, and failure;
- accumulate further capability through successful action.

## Inputs and outputs

### Major inputs

- body and cognitive functioning;
- emotion, motivation, self-efficacy, and stress;
- knowledge, education, practice, feedback, and experience;
- time, money, equipment, information, and safe opportunity;
- language, culture, mentors, peers, and organizations;
- task structure, standards, incentives, and institutional permission;
- tools, software, automation, and AI;
- prior success, failure, and recovery history.

### Major outputs

- task completion and quality;
- speed, reliability, and error profile;
- ability to handle complexity and novelty;
- learning and transfer;
- coordination and leverage;
- economic and social participation;
- increased or decreased future option sets;
- demonstrable artefacts and consequences.

## Timescales and development

```text
minutes to days: state-dependent available capability and task performance
weeks to months: practice effects, strategy acquisition, tool fluency
months to years: expertise, professional skill, judgment, portfolio, reputation
decades: cumulative education, domain shifts, adaptation, decline, and recovery
```

Capability can decay through non-use, changing environments, illness, automation, or obsolete knowledge. It can also persist externally through notes, tools, organizations, and collaborators.

## Plasticity and constraints

Capability is often highly plastic but constrained by:

- prerequisite body and cognitive function;
- developmental timing and prior education;
- practice quality and feedback;
- opportunity and exposure;
- resource and time scarcity;
- stress, motivation, and identity;
- institutional recognition and permission;
- changing technological standards;
- transfer distance between training and target task.

The existence of improvement in principle does not imply equal cost, equal ceiling, or equal access.

## Mature frameworks

### WHO ICF

ICF distinguishes body functions and structures from activities, participation, and environmental factors. Its capacity/performance orientation is useful for separating what a person could do under specified conditions from what they actually do in their ordinary environment.

Retain:

- functioning in context;
- distinction between bodily condition, activity, participation, and environment;
- accommodation and environmental support as real contributors.

Do not reduce general capability to health or disability classification.

### NIH Toolbox

NIH Toolbox measures cognitive, emotional, motor, and sensory functions. These are inputs and observable components of capability, not a full account of skilled achievement.

### OECD PIAAC and Skills Outlook

The OECD Survey of Adult Skills measures literacy, numeracy, and adaptive problem solving. PIAAC is useful because it represents capabilities needed across adult life and explicitly studies how education, background, skill use, and opportunity relate to proficiency.

The 2025 Skills Outlook also separates information-processing skills from social and emotional skills and emphasizes that background and opportunity shape their development and use.

Retain:

- literacy, numeracy, and adaptive problem solving as major acquired capability families;
- adaptive problem solving as goal achievement in changing situations using cognitive and metacognitive processes;
- skill distribution and opportunity as distinct from individual essence;
- background and environment as capability-forming conditions.

Do not treat low-stakes standardized performance as maximum personal capacity or complete practical competence.

## Measurement surfaces

- authentic work samples and portfolios;
- standardized knowledge and skill assessments;
- repeated task performance under varied conditions;
- learning curves and feedback response;
- delayed retention and novel-task transfer;
- reliability, error, speed, and recovery;
- explanation and verification quality;
- coordination and tool-use traces;
- performance with and without assistance;
- outcomes in education, work, civic life, and daily functioning.

Major failures include:

- credential substitution;
- one-task overgeneralization;
- ignoring task difficulty and opportunity;
- confusing typical effort with maximum capacity;
- measuring only assisted output;
- assuming near transfer implies broad transfer;
- equating speed with competence;
- ignoring verification, judgment, and consequence;
- excluding external tools from real capability while simultaneously attributing their entire output to the person.

## Cross-system relations

```text
Body → available energy, sensory/motor access, endurance, and recovery
Cognition → learning, representation, reasoning, control, and metacognition
Emotion/Motivation → initiation, effort, persistence, exploration, and disengagement
Behaviour/practice → skill acquisition and automatization
Identity/Values → selected domains and acceptable trade-offs
Resources → time, training, tools, safety, and repeated opportunity
Relationships → teaching, feedback, collaboration, trust, and access
Institutions → credentials, permission, standards, labour demand, and recognition
Technology → external memory, execution, simulation, scale, and substitution
Capability → resources, reputation, autonomy, and further learning opportunities
```

Capability therefore participates in cumulative advantage and disadvantage loops.

## Disputes and failure modes

- General ability, domain expertise, transferable skill, and situated performance are related but not interchangeable.
- “Potential” is often inferred from the same performances it is meant to explain.
- Real-world competence may depend on tacit knowledge and social coordination poorly captured by tests.
- Tools can simultaneously increase system capability and reduce practice of internal skill.
- Independent ability is not always the correct objective; resilience, authorship, verification, or speed may matter more.
- Institutions decide which capabilities are recognized and rewarded.

## High-value questions

1. What is the smallest capability model that distinguishes potential, available capacity, independent skill, supported performance, transfer, and recovery?
2. Which measures predict reliable real-world achievement beyond credentials and one test?
3. How should capability distributed across a person, tools, collaborators, and institutions be attributed?
4. When does assistance produce durable learning, rational delegation, dependency, or deskilling?
5. Which capabilities are broadly transferable and which remain domain-specific?
6. How do resource and opportunity constraints hide or suppress existing capability?
7. Which bottleneck—body, cognition, motivation, knowledge, resources, or context—currently limits a given outcome?

## Retention judgment

Capability is retained because deleting it forces observed performance, cognitive function, knowledge, opportunity, and tool-assisted output into one category.

Its internal component list is provisional. The core retained distinction is between what was observed once and what a person or human–tool system can reliably achieve across declared conditions.
