# H0-A — Body

## Definition and boundary

Body denotes the living biological organization that sustains, regulates, senses, moves, repairs, develops, reproduces, and eventually limits the human organism.

It includes bodily structures and functions but does not absorb:

- activity and participation, which also depend on capability and context;
- subjective experience, though bodily signals contribute to it;
- social identity or moral value;
- a medical diagnosis merely because a bodily function differs from a population norm.

WHO's ICF is an important boundary reference: body functions and structures are distinct from activities, participation, and environmental factors, while functioning and disability arise through their interaction.

## Core components

P0 retains the following functional clusters as a research index, not a new anatomy standard:

| Cluster | Core role |
|---|---|
| neural and autonomic regulation | sensing, control, coordination, arousal, movement, internal regulation |
| endocrine regulation | slower chemical coordination, metabolism, growth, stress, reproduction |
| immune and inflammatory regulation | defence, repair, surveillance, inflammatory cost |
| cardiovascular and respiratory function | circulation, oxygen and substrate delivery, waste transport |
| metabolic and digestive function | energy intake, storage, conversion, and nutrient availability |
| musculoskeletal function | force, posture, movement, protection, physical work |
| sensory function | visual, auditory, somatosensory, vestibular, olfactory, gustatory input |
| sleep, circadian, and recovery systems | temporal regulation, restoration, memory-related and metabolic support |
| reproductive and developmental function | sexual development, reproduction, pregnancy, maturation |
| integrity, pain, injury, illness, and ageing | damage detection, protection, repair, accumulated decline and adaptation |

These clusters overlap. For example, sleep is neural, endocrine, metabolic, immune, behavioural, and environmental at once.

## Functions

The Body system provides:

- viability and homeostasis;
- energy and material availability;
- sensory access to internal and external conditions;
- movement and physical action;
- arousal and sleep–wake regulation;
- stress response and recovery;
- growth, development, repair, and reproduction;
- constraints on speed, endurance, precision, attention, and participation.

The body is therefore not only a health outcome. It is an active substrate and control system for cognition, emotion, behaviour, and capability.

## Inputs and outputs

### Major inputs

- genes and developmental conditions;
- nutrition, sleep opportunity, physical activity, pathogens, toxins, injury, temperature, light, and other exposures;
- psychological stress and perceived threat;
- medication and medical intervention;
- social conditions, work demands, housing, and economic access;
- repeated behaviour and training;
- ageing and accumulated history.

### Major outputs

- energy, fatigue, pain, comfort, arousal, and interoceptive signals;
- sensory quality and motor capacity;
- physiological stress and recovery dynamics;
- disease risk and functional limitation;
- conditions that alter attention, memory, motivation, mood, and effort;
- capacity to participate in physical, cognitive, social, and economic activity.

## Timescales and development

Body processes span:

```text
milliseconds: neural and reflex activity
seconds to hours: arousal, glucose regulation, exertion, acute stress
hours to days: sleep debt, inflammation, recovery, illness progression
weeks to years: training adaptation, growth, chronic disease, reproductive change
decades: development, cumulative exposure, ageing
```

A single measurement cannot represent all these timescales. Resting state, peak capacity, ordinary daily functioning, and long-term trajectory must be distinguished.

## Plasticity and constraints

Body systems can change through development, training, recovery, treatment, environment, and behaviour. Plasticity is nevertheless constrained by:

- inherited and congenital variation;
- developmental timing;
- irreversible injury or disease;
- biological trade-offs;
- accumulated exposure;
- available resources and treatment;
- ageing.

“Modifiable” is not binary. A variable may be alterable only within a range, at high cost, or during a sensitive period.

## Mature frameworks

### WHO ICF

Retain:

- distinction among body functions and structures, activities, participation, and environment;
- functioning as person–context interaction;
- avoidance of reducing health to diagnosis alone.

Do not import the full ICF code system as the Ordivon Human ontology.

### NIH Toolbox

The NIH Toolbox provides a cross-lifespan measurement family covering motor, sensory, cognitive, and emotional function. It is useful as evidence that human functioning requires several observable domains and common measures, not as a complete theory of the body.

### NIMH RDoC arousal, regulatory, and sensorimotor systems

Retain:

- arousal, circadian rhythms, and sleep–wakefulness as interacting but distinguishable constructs;
- measurement across physiology, behaviour, and self-report;
- recognition that arousal cuts across cognition, motivation, and emotion.

Do not turn a psychiatric research matrix into a universal body model.

## Measurement surfaces

Possible observations include:

- anthropometry and body composition;
- vital signs and laboratory measures;
- sleep timing and sleep quality;
- sensory and motor tests;
- fitness, strength, mobility, and endurance;
- pain, fatigue, symptoms, and self-reported functioning;
- clinical history and diagnosis;
- wearable and environmental measurements;
- real activity and participation.

Major failures include:

- proxy substitution, such as body mass for health;
- reference-range essentialism;
- one-time measurement of a volatile state;
- confusing maximum capacity with ordinary functioning;
- device error and behavioural reactivity;
- treating diagnosis as a complete account of lived function;
- ignoring environmental accommodation and access.

## Cross-system relations

High-priority relations include:

```text
sleep and circadian regulation ↔ attention, memory, emotion, and behaviour
energy and metabolic state → effort capacity and cognitive performance
pain and illness → attention capture, motivation, participation, and income
physical activity ↔ mood, sleep, fitness, and long-term health
stress appraisal ↔ autonomic/endocrine/immune response
sensory and motor function → learning opportunities and social participation
resources and institutions → nutrition, exposure, treatment, and recovery
```

## Disputes and failure modes

- The correct top-level biological decomposition depends on the question; organ systems are not always the best causal units.
- Biomarkers can be predictive without explaining mechanism.
- Population “normality” does not define individual well-being or social value.
- Subjective fatigue and pain are not invalid merely because they lack one objective correlate.
- Health optimization can conflict with autonomy, identity, time, pleasure, or economic survival.

## High-value questions

1. Which bodily states most strongly bottleneck cognition and capability in ordinary life?
2. When should a bodily variable be represented as current state, capacity, exposure, or outcome?
3. Which functions require direct physiological measurement and which are better represented by real-world functioning?
4. How do sleep, energy, pain, stress, and recovery combine into a usable model of available human capacity?
5. Which bodily constraints are reversible, compensable by tools or context, or genuinely irreversible?

## Retention judgment

Body is retained as a top-level Human system because deleting it makes sleep, fatigue, illness, sensation, movement, recovery, development, and ageing appear as unexplained noise in cognition and behaviour.

Its internal clusters remain provisional. H0 should preserve only subdivisions that change explanation in complete cases.
