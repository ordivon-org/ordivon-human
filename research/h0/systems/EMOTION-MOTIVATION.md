# H0-C — Emotion and Motivation

## Definition and boundary

Emotion and motivation denote the systems that assign significance and value, mobilize or inhibit action, regulate effort, respond to opportunity and threat, and shape which goals receive attention over time.

P0 keeps them together because valuation, arousal, learning, and action selection are tightly coupled. It still distinguishes:

- **emotion** — coordinated changes in experience, physiology, appraisal, expression, and action tendency;
- **mood** — more diffuse and persistent affective state;
- **motivation** — processes governing direction, intensity, persistence, and disengagement of action;
- **arousal** — activation and sensitivity, not inherently positive, negative, or goal-directed;
- **preference and value commitment** — relatively persistent organization studied later under Identity and Values.

Emotion is not irrational noise, and motivation is not a moral measure of character.

## Core components

P0 uses NIMH RDoC as a mature functional reference while avoiding psychiatric reduction.

| Component | Working scope |
|---|---|
| threat and defensive systems | acute, potential, and sustained threat; vigilance; protection; avoidance |
| loss and frustrative nonreward | response to deprivation, blocked reward, failure, and loss |
| reward responsiveness | anticipation, initial response, satiation, and hedonic experience |
| reward learning | prediction error, reinforcement, habit formation, and updating |
| valuation | probability, delay, effort, cost, benefit, and subjective importance |
| approach and avoidance | orientation toward opportunities or away from danger and cost |
| arousal and activation | readiness, sensitivity, mobilization, and state regulation |
| effort and persistence | willingness to initiate, sustain, escalate, pause, or abandon action |
| goal selection and commitment | prioritization among competing motives and time horizons |
| emotion regulation | changing attention, interpretation, expression, physiology, or action |
| stress and coping | response to demands, uncertainty, uncontrollability, and resource mismatch |
| social emotion and motivation | attachment, belonging, status, shame, guilt, empathy, anger, recognition |

The list is provisional. Several entries cross into cognition, behaviour, relationships, and identity.

## Functions

Emotion and motivation systems:

- detect relevance and urgency;
- assign positive, negative, or mixed value;
- prioritize scarce attention and energy;
- prepare bodily and behavioural response;
- reinforce or weaken actions through learning;
- regulate effort, persistence, exploration, and disengagement;
- communicate internal state and social intention;
- update goals after success, failure, threat, or changed opportunity;
- coordinate short-term survival with longer-term commitments.

## Inputs and outputs

### Major inputs

- bodily state, pain, fatigue, hunger, arousal, and hormonal regulation;
- perceived threat, reward, uncertainty, control, fairness, and social evaluation;
- memory, expectation, interpretation, and causal belief;
- goals, identity, values, and prior commitments;
- relationships, norms, institutions, and material constraints;
- repeated reinforcement and learned habits.

### Major outputs

- attention and memory prioritization;
- physiological activation or withdrawal;
- approach, avoidance, persistence, aggression, care, exploration, or freezing;
- subjective feeling and confidence;
- effort allocation and time horizon;
- social signalling;
- learning-rate and decision-threshold changes;
- altered sleep, health, and recovery.

Emotion and motivation do not determine action alone. Cognitive control, norms, opportunity, habit, tools, and other people can redirect or block them.

## Timescales and development

```text
milliseconds to seconds: salience detection, startle, immediate valuation
seconds to minutes: emotional episode, action tendency, effort decision
hours to days: mood, stress recovery, unresolved threat, motivational depletion
weeks to years: learned reward structure, chronic stress, attachment, goal commitment
lifespan: temperament, social learning, identity formation, changing incentives and regulation
```

A brief emotion, persistent mood, chronic stress condition, and enduring preference require different models and measures.

## Plasticity and constraints

Emotion and motivation change through:

- learning and repeated consequences;
- cognitive reappraisal and attention control;
- social support, norms, and identity;
- bodily regulation, sleep, medication, and illness;
- changes in control, resources, threat, and opportunity;
- habit and environmental design;
- development and ageing.

Constraints include temperament, developmental history, trauma, chronic exposure, biological state, learned expectations, social position, and real material risk.

Low motivation may be a rational response to low expected value, low control, excessive cost, or repeated failure rather than a defective internal trait.

## Mature frameworks

### NIMH RDoC

RDoC distinguishes:

- Negative Valence Systems for threat, loss, and frustrative nonreward;
- Positive Valence Systems for reward responsiveness, reward learning, habit, valuation, and effort;
- Arousal/Regulatory Systems for activation, circadian rhythms, and sleep–wakefulness;
- social-process constructs relevant to affiliation and communication.

Retain:

- functional decomposition rather than one positive/negative emotion scale;
- measurement across physiology, behaviour, self-report, and experimental paradigms;
- explicit interaction with development and environment;
- separation of arousal from valence and motivation.

Do not import diagnostic framing or assume every emotion has one unique circuit or measure.

### OECD social and emotional skills

OECD work treats self-control, stress resistance, co-operation, sociability, curiosity, persistence, and related patterns as developable capacities linked to life outcomes.

Retain:

- social and emotional patterns can be consequential and partly malleable;
- family, school, peer, and socioeconomic context shape development;
- adult outcomes require more than cognitive skill.

Do not collapse emotion, motivation, personality, and skill into interchangeable self-report traits.

## Measurement surfaces

- experience sampling and structured self-report;
- behavioural choice, persistence, effort, avoidance, and help-seeking;
- physiological arousal and recovery;
- facial, vocal, postural, and interaction signals;
- reward-learning and threat paradigms;
- sleep and activity patterns;
- longitudinal goal pursuit and abandonment;
- reports from close others;
- real consequences and context.

Major failures include:

- assuming self-report directly reveals mechanism;
- inferring emotion from one facial expression;
- confusing arousal with fear or motivation;
- treating task persistence as universally good;
- ignoring incentives, control, and rational disengagement;
- using retrospective mood to reconstruct moment-by-moment state;
- pathologizing responses to genuinely harmful environments;
- moralizing effort while ignoring energy and expected return.

## Cross-system relations

```text
Body ↔ arousal, stress, sleep, pain, energy, and recovery
Cognition → appraisal, prediction, reappraisal, and goal representation
Emotion/Motivation → attention, memory encoding, decision threshold, and effort
Identity/Values → which rewards, losses, and goals matter
Relationships → attachment, belonging, status, threat, support, and co-regulation
Resources/Institutions → control, security, incentives, exposure, and opportunity
Behaviour → reinforcement history and future motivational state
Capability → expected success, self-efficacy, and available strategies
```

## Disputes and failure modes

- Discrete-emotion, dimensional-valence/arousal, appraisal, constructionist, and predictive-processing accounts emphasize different explanatory units.
- Self-control may reflect conflict, habit, environment, skill, or value alignment rather than one internal resource.
- “Intrinsic” and “extrinsic” motivation are often mixed in real action.
- Persistence can become escalation of commitment; disengagement can be adaptive.
- Stress can mobilize short-term action while damaging long-term functioning.
- Similar behaviour may arise from different emotions and motives.

## High-value questions

1. Which distinctions among emotion, mood, arousal, motivation, and value are necessary in complete human cases?
2. How do expected value, control, effort cost, and uncertainty determine action initiation and persistence?
3. When does stress improve acute performance, and when does it degrade learning, health, and judgment?
4. How do body state and social environment alter motivation within the same person?
5. Which regulation strategies transfer across contexts rather than merely suppress expression?
6. How should rational disengagement be distinguished from avoidance, helplessness, or incapacity?

## Retention judgment

Emotion and Motivation are retained because deleting them leaves salience, value, threat, reward, effort, persistence, avoidance, stress, and goal competition unexplained.

Their joint top-level placement is provisional. H0 may later separate stable value organization, episodic emotion, and motivational control if complete cases require it.
