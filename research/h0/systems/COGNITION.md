# H0-B — Cognition

## Definition and boundary

Cognition denotes the processes by which a person detects, selects, represents, stores, retrieves, transforms, predicts, and controls information relevant to action.

Cognition is not identical to:

- intelligence as one latent score;
- knowledge accumulated through education;
- motivation to use a capacity;
- observed task performance;
- rationality or moral correctness;
- capability in a real environment.

A person can possess a cognitive capacity but fail to express it because of fatigue, threat, low motivation, unfamiliar context, missing knowledge, poor measurement, or inadequate tools.

## Core components

P0 composes two mature references: NIMH RDoC Cognitive Systems and the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery.

| Component | Working scope |
|---|---|
| perception | constructing usable representations from sensory input |
| attention | selecting, sustaining, orienting, and reallocating processing |
| working memory | temporarily maintaining and updating task-relevant information |
| declarative and episodic memory | encoding, retaining, and retrieving facts and events |
| semantic knowledge and language | learned representations, comprehension, expression, and symbol use |
| processing speed | rate of performing selected elementary operations under defined conditions |
| cognitive control / executive function | goal maintenance, inhibition, switching, updating, planning, and conflict resolution |
| reasoning and problem representation | deriving relations, constructing models, inference, and solution search |
| learning | durable change from experience, instruction, feedback, and practice |
| metacognition | monitoring knowledge, confidence, error, strategy, and progress |

Creativity, imagination, numeracy, literacy, and adaptive problem solving are not omitted, but their placement is mixed: they combine basic cognitive processes with learned knowledge, strategies, motivation, and context. They are therefore treated primarily as capabilities built from cognition rather than elementary cognitive components.

## Functions

Cognition supports:

- detecting relevant conditions;
- representing current and possible worlds;
- retaining history beyond the present moment;
- learning regularities and skills;
- predicting consequences;
- selecting and controlling action;
- communicating through language and symbols;
- monitoring uncertainty and correcting error;
- transferring prior knowledge to novel situations.

## Inputs and outputs

### Major inputs

- sensory and interoceptive signals;
- prior knowledge and memory;
- goals, values, threat, reward, and emotion;
- sleep, energy, illness, age, and neurodevelopment;
- education, practice, language, culture, and tools;
- task structure and information environment.

### Major outputs

- beliefs and representations;
- recalled information;
- predictions and judgments;
- plans, decisions, explanations, and strategies;
- attention allocation;
- confidence and error signals;
- learned changes that contribute to capability.

Cognitive output does not directly equal action. Motivation, opportunity, social constraint, and bodily capacity intervene.

## Timescales and development

```text
milliseconds to seconds: perception, attention shifts, inhibition
seconds to minutes: working memory, reasoning episodes, task control
hours to days: encoding, consolidation, fatigue, state-dependent learning
weeks to years: knowledge, expertise, strategy, language, skill acquisition
lifespan: neurodevelopment, education, experience, ageing, disease
```

Fluid and crystallized aspects may follow different developmental trajectories. Cross-sectional age differences also mix ageing, cohort, education, and selection effects.

## Plasticity and constraints

Cognitive performance changes with:

- development and ageing;
- education and deliberate practice;
- task familiarity and strategy;
- sleep, stress, illness, and medication;
- feedback and motivation;
- language and culture;
- external representations and tools.

Constraints include limited attention, working-memory capacity, interference, noisy evidence, finite time, accumulated knowledge, and biological condition.

Training gains may remain task-specific. Improvement on a practiced test is not sufficient evidence of broad cognitive transfer.

## Mature frameworks

### NIMH RDoC Cognitive Systems

RDoC identifies constructs including attention, perception, declarative memory, language, cognitive control, and working memory, studied across genes, molecules, cells, circuits, physiology, behaviour, self-report, and paradigms.

Retain:

- multiple interacting constructs rather than one cognition score;
- multiple units of analysis;
- developmental and environmental context;
- explicit overlap across domains.

Do not import RDoC's psychiatric research purpose or full matrix as a universal ontology.

### NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery

The NIH Toolbox measures executive function, attention, episodic memory, language, processing speed, and working memory across much of the lifespan.

Retain:

- distinction among cognitive domains;
- cross-lifespan comparison and psychometric validation;
- separation of fluid and crystallized composites where useful.

Do not mistake a brief battery for a complete map of cognition or real-world competence.

### OECD adult skills

OECD PIAAC assesses literacy, numeracy, and adaptive problem solving. These are important information-processing capabilities for adult life, but they combine cognition with learned knowledge, engagement, culture, and task context.

Their placement helps enforce the cognition–capability boundary.

## Measurement surfaces

- controlled cognitive tasks;
- accuracy, latency, and error pattern;
- learning curves and retention tests;
- transfer and novel-task performance;
- confidence and calibration;
- language and knowledge assessments;
- process traces and strategy reports;
- real-world task outcomes;
- neurophysiological and imaging measures where justified.

Major failures include:

- treating test score as an intrinsic essence;
- speed–accuracy trade-off blindness;
- practice and familiarity effects;
- cultural and language loading;
- low motivation in low-stakes tests;
- equating neural activation with a unique cognitive process;
- assuming near transfer implies far transfer;
- conflating knowledge with fluid processing.

## Cross-system relations

```text
Body → arousal, energy, sensation, processing stability, and memory consolidation
Emotion/Motivation → salience, attention, learning rate, effort, and decision threshold
Cognition → appraisal, emotion regulation, planning, and behaviour
Education and practice → knowledge, strategies, automatization, and expertise
Tools → external memory, representation, search, calculation, and simulation
Context → task demands, information quality, incentives, and cognitive load
Capability = cognition combined with knowledge, motivation, body, tools, and context
```

## Disputes and failure modes

- Broad intelligence factors summarize covariance but do not eliminate the need for mechanisms and domain profiles.
- Executive function definitions and task purity remain contested.
- Laboratory tasks often have weak correspondence with everyday functioning.
- Cognitive constructs overlap; a task rarely measures only one process.
- Metacognitive confidence can be informative, biased, or strategically expressed.
- External tools may reduce internal demand while increasing real system capability.

## High-value questions

1. Which cognitive distinctions materially improve explanation beyond a general performance factor?
2. How should knowledge, reasoning, learning rate, and transfer be separated?
3. Which laboratory measures predict real work and independent adaptation?
4. How do body state and emotion alter available cognition within the same person?
5. When does tool use extend cognition, substitute for it, or change what should be learned internally?
6. What evidence distinguishes temporary performance support from durable cognitive change?

## Retention judgment

Cognition is retained because deleting it collapses perception, memory, learning, reasoning, control, and metacognition into observed behaviour without explaining how information becomes action.

Its subcomponents remain empirical constructs, not immutable mental modules.
